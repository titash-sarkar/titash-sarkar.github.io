A Working Approximation

I build machine learning systems for enterprise — shaping how intelligence is expressed is core to my work. A neural network begins with an arbitrary configuration. Through batches of data and epochs of updates, its parameters move across a complex loss landscape, until it discovers useful representations. This bears an uncanny resemblance to my own pursuit of understanding intelligence.

Fuzzy boundaries are my favourite territory: where physics gives way to philosophy, mathematics defies perception, and computation starts to look like thought. Precise equations describe everyday reality, yet leave profound questions unsettled - Is the universe fundamentally discrete or continuous — like grains of sand forming an apparently seamless beach? How does subjective experience emerge from physical matter — Is consciousness entirely a product of physical processes? Mathematics lets us explore dimensions and infinities beyond intuition. How can billions of numbers, shaped by a relatively simple learning process, echo aspects of human intelligence forged through millions of years of evolution?

These questions seem to belong to different worlds, yet they keep returning to common themes: structure, emergence, information and complexity. Followed far enough, they may converge on a deeper fabric connecting intelligence, perception and reality.

Our perception is a model, not reality: It is constrained by our sensory apparatus, cognitive architecture, memory and evolutionary history. We see only a narrow band of electromagnetic spectrum as visible light. We experience time flowing in one direction only. Our intuitions about probability, dimensions, scale and causality are often unreliable.

The known unknowns already dwarf us. Could an intelligence born of sand help us discover the gap between reality and our models of it? Can it reveal patterns beyond direct human observation — not because it is free of limitations, but because its limitations are different from ours? Can artificial intelligence bring us closer to reality?

Could we develop a symbiotic relationship with silicon intelligence to explore the unknown unknowns — expanding what we can understand, imagine and do.

And in this pursuit, can we expand our capabilities without surrendering our agency?

The Spirit of Inquiry

Ayn Rand taught me to trust reason; Kafka and Dostoevsky to watch where it breaks. Feynman made the fundamentals feel accessible. Sagan made the universe feel worth questioning. Books, lectures, papers, long conversations and unexpected encounters with ideas have all shaped the questions I continue to pursue.

I write to understand and build things from first principles, not to instruct. If an essay here makes you see something you had only ever memorised, it worked.
