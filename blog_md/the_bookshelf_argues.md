# The Literature of Ideas

Growing up, I read like most kids. Enid Blyton's boarding schools came first: the Naughtiest Girl, Malory Towers, St Clare's. Then the adventurers, the Secret Seven and the Famous Five; the mystery solvers, Nancy Drew and Agatha Christie; and the magical worlds of Hogwarts and Middle-earth.

At the impressionable age of 15, I read The Fountainhead by Ayn Rand and it has had a defining influence on my life. Howard Roark thinks for himself, and other people's opinions never enter his judgment. He dynamites his building rather than let it stand compromised. The book ushered me into an adult world where I could stand by my own convictions and principles, even if the rest disagreed.
Of course the next had to be Atlas Shrugged and thus began my exploration of Russian novelists and European philosophers.

While the philosophers dealt with life on Earth — emotion, how to be, the frameworks of morality and justice — what about life beyond it? Carl Sagan's Cosmos opened my mind to vastness and made me ask whether any of it matters at all: the sheer scale of the universe, the randomness of things, the arrogance of our intelligence, the inconsequence of our being. Asimov and Herbert carried those questions to other worlds, while physicists still argue over whether this one is even real.

None of these gave me answers I could keep. Each left a question instead, and the questions turned out to last longer. If one of them finds you at the right age, the way Roark found me at fifteen, it might do the same.
